# AI Game Engine: Decisions and Reasoning (2026-09-19)

**What we're building and on what.** An AI-first game engine for serious creators working alone or in teams of one to five: the interface is the game plus an AI window, and agents do the coding, assets, debugging and publishing. We settled on building on a thin Godot 4 fork rather than from scratch, because the renderer, physics, animation and export pipeline are hardware-facing rather than human-era, and the agent-facing layer (observe the running game, act on it, preserve state across edits, headless tests) doesn't exist anywhere and we'd build it regardless. Most engine work goes in as extensions against a prebuilt Godot binary so one command builds the engine layer, harness and game in seconds. The game language is C#, not C++ or a TypeScript core, because the agent loop and crash readability matter more than the model's language preference. We dogfood one real 3D game at a time in the same monorepo, starting with something shippable in months.

**Hardware and licensing.** The MacBook Neo is an A18 Pro with 8 GB RAM and a fanless chassis that throttles after about 30 minutes, which puts it in Switch 2 handheld class and well above Switch 1. The honest target is the Switch 1 library at 60 fps native and Switch 2 handheld-class with upscaling, and memory discipline matters more than peak throughput. On licensing we rejected MIT, and the Godot numbers back it: games on Godot approach $1B in revenue while the foundation took in about EUR 466k in 2023, with the creators capturing value only through a separate company (W4 Games). Source-available lets us charge, keeps the code public for the technical evangelists who drive adoption, and for EB-1A purposes gives independently verifiable evidence in the middling-success case. Cursor is closed and proves closed can win, so the license is a hedge, not a strategy.

**Business model and why now.** Two tiers: a membership with hosted AI plus the things a key can't buy (asset generation, cloud builds, publishing, multiplayer), and a free tier with bring-your-own-key that must be the full product. Both pay a 5% royalty per game above a threshold (proposed $200k; argued for $500k to $1M), and the terms for a given engine version must never change retroactively, which is the Unity lesson. Unreal's engine business is around $275M a year with Fortnite subsidizing it, so the subscription is the main revenue and the royalty is the long tail. The industry context is flat player spending, exploding costs, $300M six-year AAA budgets and years of layoffs. The thesis is that AI collapses content, iteration and coordination cost so small teams ship big games. The first game exists to prove exactly that.

## Decisions at a glance

- Core: thin Godot 4 fork, engine work as GDExtensions, upstream general fixes
- Game language: typed GDScript for gameplay, C++ extensions for engine work and hot paths (C# dropped)
- License: source-available (readable, no redistribution of a competing product), terms fixed per engine version
- Pricing: membership (hosted AI + asset gen + builds + publishing + multiplayer) or free with BYOK, both with 5% royalty per game on lifetime gross above $200k, no royalty in any quarter under $10k (Unreal terms, lower threshold). Licensor: Ahmet Ilten personally. License file: FSL-1.1-ALv2 verbatim + Product Royalty Terms.
- Target hardware: Mac only for the tool, optimized for Apple Silicon like a console; MacBook Neo is the floor; Switch 1 library native, Switch 2 handheld-class with upscaling. Games ship as DMG from your own site and via Mac/iOS App Store; other exports kept as checkboxes
- Process: one monorepo, one build command, one dogfood game at a time
- First step: prove an agent on stock Godot can take a one-line prompt to a running third-person game and iterate on it
